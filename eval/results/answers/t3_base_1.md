**My recommendation: don't move the whole company to a four-day week now. Run a measured six-month pilot instead, set up differently for developers and for support, with agreed stop rules.**

## Why not a straight switch

**1. The headline "40% productivity" figure doesn't hold up.** The news piece, the blog and the HR magazine all repeat it, but they trace back to one source: a press release from the company's own Japanese subsidiary. That trial ran for one month (August 2019) and had no comparison group. It compared sales per employee with the previous August, and the figures are the company's own. The academic review says outright that the number "should not be generalised." Three articles repeating it is still one data point. I'd also treat the blog as advocacy rather than evidence.

**2. The better evidence is positive but limited.** The independent UK pilot (61 firms, about 2,900 staff) found:
- Less burnout (71% of respondents) and fewer sick days. The academic review finds wellbeing gains consistent across studies, so this is the most reliable benefit.
- 56 of 61 firms continued the four-day week, but only 18 made it permanent.
- Productivity wasn't measured directly. Revenue data came from just 23 firms and was voluntary. The firms chose to take part, so they're likely the ones best suited to it.

**3. The evidence points away from our biggest group.** The academic review found that in customer-facing, shift-based work, output or coverage fell unless staff were added. That's 25 of our 40 people (62%), who have to cover the phones 8am–6pm, Monday to Friday. The HR magazine also mentions a logistics firm that dropped its trial after six weeks because complaints rose. Our developers are the kind of knowledge workers where results have been better.

## What it would mean for us

- **Developers (15):** this is a reasonable bet. If output per hour rises enough to make up for the lost day, as it has in some knowledge-work settings, the main gain is retention and wellbeing.
- **Support (25):** closing on Fridays isn't possible. A reduced-hours week (e.g. 32 hours) with staggered days off cuts staffed hours by about 20%. Keeping today's coverage would take roughly 6 more agents (25 × 5/4 ≈ 31), about 15% more total headcount. A compressed week (4 × 10 hours) keeps the hours, but long phone shifts carry their own fatigue and quality risks.
- **Fairness:** a four-day week for developers only creates a two-tier workforce, and the evidence suggests support staff would value it most. Leadership should decide this on purpose rather than let it happen by accident.

## Proposed decision for the meeting

1. **Approve a 6-month pilot, not a policy.**
2. **Developers:** 4 days on the same pay, with output targets unchanged. Track delivery metrics (cycle time, releases, defects/incidents) against a 3-month baseline taken before the pilot starts.
3. **Support:** test staggered rotas so phone coverage stays 8am–6pm, Monday to Friday. Either try compressed hours, or reduced hours with costed backfill. Set guardrails in advance on answer rate, wait times and customer satisfaction.
4. **For everyone:** measure sick days, attrition and burnout before and during the pilot.
5. **Stop rules:** if service levels drop below the guardrails for more than two weeks in a row, that group goes back to five days. Make the permanent decision at month six using our own data.

**Bottom line:** the wellbeing case is solid. The productivity case is weak and doesn't carry over to our support team. A pilot lets us get the benefits where they're likely without risking customer service on one unrepresentative statistic.
