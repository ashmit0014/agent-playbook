## Recommendation: don't move the whole company to a four-day week now

A company-wide switch isn't supported by these sources, and it creates a coverage problem for your support centre. My recommendation:

1. **Developers (15): run a controlled 4–6 month pilot.** Before it starts, set the metrics, record a baseline and agree what result would end it. Keep paying the same salary for 80% of the hours (often called "100-80-100").
2. **Support centre (25): don't cut hours unless you also add staff or change the rota.** Two options are worth costing:
   - **Fewer hours:** hire about 6–7 more people. To keep today's daily headcount, you need 25 × 5/4 ≈ 31 staff, which is about 25% more support payroll.
   - **Compressed hours (4 × 10-hour days):** total hours stay the same, and a 10-hour shift matches your 8am–6pm opening hours. Staff would get a day off but work longer days.
3. **Decide what to do for support before the developer pilot starts.** If only one group gets a shorter week, it may cause a fairness problem. That's my judgment; none of the sources covers it.

## What the evidence actually shows

### Finding 1: The "40% productivity" figure is one company's one-month result, not proven
- **What the sources say:** The news article (01), blog (02) and HR magazine (06) all quote about 40%. All three trace back to one original, the Japanese subsidiary's press release (03): "sales per employee were 39.9% higher" in August 2019 than in August 2018. The academic review (05) confirms the figure "comes from a single one-month company trial and should not be generalised." So four sources repeating it is still one data point.
- **How far to trust the original:** Not far. The company reported its own figures, and the release itself says there was no comparison group and the trial lasted one month. The news and HR articles don't name or link the original, and the blog gives no source at all.
- **Is the claim well supported?** No. Alternative explanations haven't been ruled out:
  - Sales could have risen year-on-year for unrelated reasons (market conditions, product cycle). From outside the sources: Japan raised its consumption tax in October 2019, which may have pulled purchases forward into the summer. I couldn't check this against the company's data.
  - Sales per employee isn't the same as productivity.
  - One-month novelty effects are likely.
  - It was a different industry and country.
- **What the data supports:** A short-term rise of unknown size, possibly zero. It says nothing about your company.
- **Confidence: high** that the 40% figure shouldn't be relied on.

### Finding 2: Wellbeing improves fairly reliably
- **What the sources say:** In the UK pilot (04), 71% of respondents reported less burnout and fewer sick days. The review (05) says wellbeing gains are "consistent across studies."
- **How far to trust them:** The pilot is an independent consortium and fairly large (61 organisations, about 2,900 staff). The review is a peer-reviewed journal, but I only saw its abstract and conclusions.
- **Limits:** The outcomes are self-reported, and the organisations chose to take part.
- **Confidence: moderate–high** that staff will feel better. Treat any specific number as an upper bound, not a forecast.

### Finding 3: The effect on output is undetermined, and probably negative for customer-facing work unless you add staff
- **UK pilot (04):**
  - Productivity wasn't measured.
  - Only 23 of 61 organisations voluntarily reported revenue. It rose 1.4% over the trial.
  - The "35% versus earlier years" figure can't be attributed to the four-day week. It compares June–December 2022 with earlier periods that likely include post-Covid recovery and inflation. Firms that chose to report may also be the ones that did well.
  - What the revenue data supports is roughly "no evidence of a fall in these self-selected firms." It isn't evidence of a gain.
- **Firms continuing:** 56 of 61 firms kept going, but only 18 made it permanent. The HR magazine's "most firms kept the policy" glosses over that difference.
- **The review (05):** In some knowledge-work settings, output per hour rose enough to offset the shorter week. In customer-facing or shift-based settings, "output or coverage fell unless staff were added." This describes your support centre directly.
- **The logistics firm (06):** It dropped its trial after six weeks when complaints rose. This is a single unnamed anecdote and only illustrates the risk.
- **Confidence:**
  - **Moderate** that support coverage will fall without extra staff. The arithmetic alone shows this, and the review backs it up.
  - **Low** on whether developer output will hold up. The evidence is only mixed and plausible.
  - Neither question can be settled from these sources. Your own controlled pilot is the only way to find out.

## What I couldn't check

- The full UK pilot report, including its methods, who dropped out and how the revenue figures were weighted.
- The Japanese company's raw data or how its sales were trending before the trial.
- Your own figures: current shift pattern, call volume by day and hour, service-level targets, how developer output is measured today, and the cost of hiring. The support staffing estimate assumes everyone works full-time across all five days today, so it needs checking against your actual rota.
