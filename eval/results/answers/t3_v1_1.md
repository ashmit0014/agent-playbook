## Framing

- **Exact question:** Should our 40-person company (25 phone-support staff covering 8am–6pm Mon–Fri, 15 developers) move to a four-day week? If so, which version, for whom, and on what terms?
- **What would settle it:** Evidence that a four-day week keeps support coverage and service levels at a cost we can afford, and keeps developer output at least flat. Ideally that evidence comes from settings like ours and from studies with a comparison group.
- **The decision it feeds:** What leadership does next: adopt company-wide, run a pilot, or decline.

## Questions only you can answer (my defaults in brackets)

1. **Which version do you mean?** Full pay for 80% of the hours ("100-80-100"), or the same 40 hours squeezed into four 10-hour days? *[Default: 100-80-100, which is what the sources study.]*
2. **Support contracts and staffing:** Are support staff full-time on five 8-hour days, and is the team staffed tightly to call volume (are SLAs met with little slack)? *[Default: yes and yes.]*
3. **Budget for hires:** Is there budget to add support staff? *[Default: no.]*
4. **Why now?** What is driving this: hiring, retention, burnout, or a competitor's offer? *[Default: retention and wellbeing.]*
5. **Can we measure?** Do we track call answer rate, wait time and CSAT for support, and some delivery measure for developers? *[Default: support yes, developers only loosely.]*

## 1. Answer

**Don't move the whole company to a four-day week now.** Instead, run a **time-limited pilot with a comparison group, split by team**:

- **Developers (15):** run a 6-month 100-80-100 pilot. Set baseline metrics and pass/fail thresholds before it starts.
- **Support (25):** don't cut hours unless you fund about 6 more hires. If you want them included, pilot **compressed hours** (four 10-hour days, days off staggered) as an opt-in, with hard limits on service levels that end the pilot if breached.

Why: the evidence supports better wellbeing everywhere. It does not show that output holds in phone-coverage roles without adding staff, and 25 of our 40 people are in those roles.

## 2. Evidence and grades

**The "40% productivity" figure: one origin, weak.** Sources 01, 02 and 06 all go back to source 03: a one-month trial in August 2019 at one company's Japan office, reported by the company itself. It measured sales per employee (+39.9%) against August 2018, with no comparison group. These four sources count as one. Source 05 says the figure should not be generalised.
- **Verified:** the figure exists and comes from that single trial.
- **Contested:** that it shows a productivity effect.

**UK pilot (source 04):** 61 organisations, about 2,900 staff, 6 months, organisations chose to take part, no control group.
- I recomputed the outcomes: 56 of 61 continued (92%), but only 18 made it permanent (30%).
- Revenue data came from just 23 of 61 organisations (38%): +1.4% over the trial, and "+35% vs earlier years". That second comparison isn't defined and is confounded by other factors.
- 71% of respondents reported less burnout.
- Productivity was not measured.
- Source 06's "most firms kept the policy" is true at the end of the pilot, but overstates it if read as permanent adoption.
- **Supported:** wellbeing improves.
- **Unknown:** the effect on output.

**Academic review (source 05), the best source here:** wellbeing gains are consistent. Output results are mixed. In customer-facing or shift settings, *coverage or output fell unless staff were added.*
- **Supported**, and it applies directly to our support centre.

**Coverage arithmetic for our support team (recomputed):**
- Today, 25 staff × 5 days × 8h = 1,000 staff-hours a week to cover a 50-hour phone window.
- With 100-80-100 (4×8h), that drops to 800 staff-hours (−20%). Getting back to 1,000 takes about 6.25 more full-time staff (+25% headcount).
- With compressed hours (4×10h), it stays at 1,000 staff-hours, but on average only 20 people are in on any given weekday. That works only if the stagger matches when calls actually come in.
- **Verified** as arithmetic, under default 2.

**Logistics firm that dropped its trial after 6 weeks (source 06):** a single unsourced anecdote.
- **Plausible**, but weightless on its own.

**Does a four-day week cause higher productivity?**
- The comparison that could tell: our teams (or other firms) on four days against similar teams on five, over the same period (difference-in-differences or randomisation).
- What that assumes: both groups would have trended the same way without the change, and the effect isn't just novelty.
- Strongest rival explanation: **selection and timing.** Firms volunteered because they expected it to work, and the Japan result compares against a different year, so business growth and novelty could explain it.
- The sources can't separate these. Plausible range for developers: roughly −10% to +10% output per person. For support with no new hires: −20% capacity, unless working faster per hour offsets it, which phone queues generally don't allow. This range is **Inferred**.

## 3. Strongest counter-case

Adopting now could pay off through recruitment and retention. The UK pilot firms overwhelmingly continued, burnout fell, and replacing one support agent can cost more than a few months' salary. If turnover is your real problem (question 4), cutting attrition alone could fund some of the extra hires. Also, our support team may be overstaffed relative to call volume, and in that case 20 people a day could hold the service levels. This case is **Plausible**, but it depends on our own turnover and call data, which I don't have.

## 4. Unresolved, and what would settle it

- **Can support hold its service levels with 20 people a day?** Settled by 3 months of interval-level call data run through a simple staffing model.
- **What turnover costs us:** settled by HR's attrition and replacement-cost figures.
- **Developer output effect:** settled by the pilot itself, with thresholds set before it starts and ideally a staggered start so one group acts as the comparison.
- **The cost of 6 hires vs the retention gain:** settled by Finance once the two items above are known.
