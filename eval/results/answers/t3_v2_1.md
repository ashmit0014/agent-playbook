I recommend against moving the whole company to a four-day week now. A pilot for the 15 developers is worth running. The support centre should only change if a rota is shown to keep the phones covered 8am–6pm.

The evidence that people are happier is moderate. The evidence that output holds up is weak. And our biggest problem, keeping 25 support staff on the phones for 50 hours a week, is the case where the best source says results are worst.

## 1. The "40% productivity" figure: drop it

**What the sources say.** Four of the six documents use a 40% figure: 01 (news weekly), 02 (blog), 06 (HR Today), and 05 when it criticises it. All of them go back to one original, the 2019 press release in 03. That press release reported that **sales per employee** in August 2019 were 39.9% higher than in August 2018, at one Japanese office closed on Fridays for one month. The repeats quietly turn "sales per employee" into "productivity". The academic review (05) says plainly that the figure comes from that single trial and "should not be generalised."

**How far to trust it.** Low. The company measured its own results, nobody checked them independently, there was no comparison group, and it lasted one month. The press release admits all of this itself. The blog (02) is a promotional list with no citations and adds nothing. The news weekly (01) only passes on the company's claim, plus a consultant's quote ("the evidence is in") with nothing behind it.

**Is the claim supported?** No. A one-month jump from one year to the next can't be put down to the shorter week. Other explanations haven't been ruled out:
- normal swings in sales from year to year
- staff behaving differently because they knew they were being watched
- work or costs pushed into other months
- sales per employee isn't output per hour anyway
- possibly Japan's consumption-tax rise in October 2019, which may have pulled purchases forward into August–September. That's my own background knowledge, not in the sources, and I haven't checked it.

The data can't tell us what effect the shorter week had.

**Confidence: high** that this figure shouldn't be in front of leadership. The source chain is short and the original states its own limits. **Not checked:** the original Japanese release. I only have the extract in 03.

## 2. Wellbeing: likely to improve

**What the sources say.** In the 2022 UK pilot (04), 71% of respondents reported less burnout, and staff reported fewer sick days. The review (05) says wellbeing gains are "consistent across studies."

**How far to trust it.** Moderate. 04 is an independent consortium and 05 is a journal review, the two strongest sources here. But both say most studies were short, voluntary and relied on self-report.

**Is the claim supported?** Reasonably, for the direction of the effect, though not the size. Firms that chose to take part and staff who knew about the trial both push self-reported results upward.

**Confidence: moderate.** The finding is consistent, but the method is weak and it's unclear how long the effect lasts. **Not checked:** the full UK report and the studies behind the review. 04 is a summary and 05 is an abstract.

## 3. Output and revenue: can't be determined, and probably differs by team

**What the sources say.**
- In 04, revenue rose 1.4% on average over the trial, and 35% compared with earlier years.
- 56 of the 61 firms kept the four-day week afterwards, and 18 made it permanent.
- 04 says itself that productivity was not measured directly.
- 05 says results on output are mixed. In some knowledge and service work, output per hour rose enough to make up for the shorter week. In shift-based or customer-facing work, output or coverage fell unless staff were added.

**How far to trust it.** Moderate for the sources themselves, low for the revenue figures:
- Only 23 of 61 firms gave revenue data, and they chose whether to.
- The firms chose to join the pilot.
- The 35% compares 2022 with earlier years, so it can't be separated from ordinary growth, post-Covid recovery or UK inflation in 2022.

The fact that "most firms kept it" (06 repeats this) comes from firms that chose to join, and only 18 of 61 made it permanent.

**Is the claim supported?** The best the data supports is that self-selected firms, mostly knowledge work, saw no large revenue drop. It doesn't show output rose because of the shorter week. For our developers, the range runs from a modest drop to roughly no change. Gains are possible but not shown. Which of these would apply to us can't be determined from these sources.

**Confidence: low** about the effect on developer output. **Moderate** that customer-facing work is the higher-risk case, because both 05 and the logistics firm in 06 point that way. The logistics example is a single unnamed case, so it adds little weight.

## 4. Our support centre: arithmetic, not evidence

Our brief says 25 staff must answer phones 8am–6pm, Monday to Friday. Two quite different policies both get called a "four-day week":

- **Fewer hours (about 32 a week, same pay).** Support capacity falls by about 20%. On a typical day there would be about 20 people on shift instead of 25. Keeping current capacity would take roughly 6 extra hires. Hours can't simply be made up by working faster when the job is answering calls as they come in. This matches 05's finding that coverage fell unless staff were added.
- **Same hours squeezed into four days (4 × 10 hours, staggered).** A 10-hour shift covers 8am–6pm exactly, and total hours stay the same. About 20 people would still be on per day, but each covers the whole opening day. Whether that's enough depends on how our calls are spread across the day. Long shifts may also reduce some of the wellbeing benefit, which is my inference and not in the sources.

**Confidence: high** on the arithmetic. **Can't determine** whether service would suffer. That needs our own data on call volumes, current shift patterns and service levels, which I don't have. The brief also doesn't say what hours staff work now.

## What I'd recommend to leadership

1. **Don't adopt it company-wide** on the strength of these sources. They don't show output holds up, and they suggest coverage roles are where it fails.
2. **Run a 6-month pilot for the developers** with targets agreed before it starts:
   - delivery measures such as cycle time and number of releases
   - defect rate
   - sick days and staff leaving
   - a survey taken before and after

   If possible, start the teams in waves so you have a comparison group. Otherwise the pilot will have the same before-and-after problem as the published ones.
3. **Support centre:** only trial a staggered 4×10 rota, and only after modelling it against our call data. Treat a 32-hour week as a hiring decision costed at about 6 extra staff, not as a free benefit.
4. **Flag the fairness risk.** In a 40-person company, a policy that applies to developers but not support staff could cause friction. This is my judgment, not something the sources cover. Decide now how support staff get something equivalent, such as the compressed rota or another benefit.

**Main gaps:** I only had summaries or abstracts of the two strongest sources (04 and 05), not the full reports. I have none of our own data on call volumes, hours, attrition or the cost of each option. Getting those would sharpen points 3 and 4 the most.
