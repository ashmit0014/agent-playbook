**My recommendation: do not move the whole company to a four-day week now.** The evidence doesn't support it for your support centre, which is 25 of your 40 staff. A measured pilot for the 15 developers is reasonable.

## The answer for leadership

1. **No company-wide four-day week (32 hours, same pay).** Confidence: **high.**
2. **Developers: run a 6-month pilot** with targets agreed before it starts. Confidence: **moderate** that it's worth trying, **low** on whether output will hold.
3. **Support centre: don't cut hours unless you accept the cost.** If you want to offer something, trial a compressed or staggered rota (same hours over fewer days) and judge it on service measures. Confidence: **high** that cutting hours without hiring will reduce phone coverage.

## Where the "40% productivity" figure comes from

Four sources mention it: newsweekly 01, blog 02, HR Today 06 and the academic review 05. They all trace back to **one press release (03)**. The review says so directly.

What that press release actually reports:
- **The measure was sales per employee, not productivity.** It compares August 2019 with August 2018.
- **The trial lasted one month and had no comparison group.** The release says this itself.
- **The company reported its own figures,** and they were never independently checked.
- **Other explanations aren't ruled out:** the company's normal year-on-year growth, sales landing in different months, staff working harder because the trial was new, and the fact that it was summer.

**Conclusion:** the figure can't tell you how much the four-day week itself caused, even at that company. You shouldn't apply it to yours. Source 01 calls it a productivity jump of 40% and a consultant there says "the evidence is in." Neither is supported. Source 06's "gains of up to 40%... in trials" makes one trial sound like several.

## What the stronger evidence supports

**Wellbeing improves (moderate–high confidence).** The review calls wellbeing gains consistent across studies, and the UK pilot reports less burnout and fewer sick days. However, most of this is self-reported. The pilot gives "71% of respondents" without saying how many responded. Short pilots may also capture a novelty effect.

**Output is mixed and depends on the type of work (moderate confidence).** According to the review:
- In knowledge work, output per hour sometimes rises enough to make up for fewer hours.
- In shift-based or customer-facing work, output or coverage fell unless staff were added.

That split matches your company almost exactly.

**The UK pilot's results are weaker than they look:**
- **Revenue up 1.4%:** only 23 of 61 firms reported revenue, voluntarily. Firms doing badly may have chosen not to report. And 1.4% over six months with no control group is consistent with "no clear change in either direction."
- **Revenue up 35% compared with earlier years:** in 2022 that comparison mixes in inflation and post-COVID recovery, so it can't be credited to the four-day week.
- **"Most firms kept it":** 56 of 61 continued, but only 18 made it permanent. Source 06's wording overstates the permanent figure.
- **The firms chose to take part,** and productivity was never measured directly. The report says both of these itself.

**The most the evidence supports:** in self-selected knowledge-work firms, a four-day week did not obviously damage revenue over six months, and staff felt better. A reliable size of effect on output can't be determined from these sources.

## Applying it to your company

**Support centre (25 staff, phones staffed 8am–6pm, Monday to Friday):**
- Today that is about 1,000 staffed hours a week, or about 20 people on the phones at any time.
- At 32 hours each it drops to 800 hours, or about 16 on the phones: **a 20% cut.**
- To make up for that, each agent would need to handle about 25% more per hour. Nothing in these sources shows that for phone support, where call volume is set by customers.
- Keeping current coverage would take roughly **6 more hires**, about 25% more support payroll.
- The alternative is four 10-hour days that cover the 8am–6pm window, staggered so the phones stay staffed. This keeps hours the same. The sources don't separate results for this compressed format, so treat it as untested.

**Developers (15 staff):** this is the setting where the review found gains could offset fewer hours. A pilot is reasonable if:
- you measure a baseline before it starts (delivery measures, incidents, response times for support escalations),
- you stagger days off so someone is always available to support staff,
- you set a review point and a rule for stopping.

**My own judgement, not from the sources:** giving the four-day week to developers but not support staff could cause resentment. Plan for that, for example by offering support staff the compressed rota or another benefit.
