**The Thirteenth**

The supply boat came on Thursdays, and this was a Wednesday, so the letter should not have been there at all.

Maren found it at the foot of the stairs when she came down from the lamp at a quarter past six. Her hands still smelled of paraffin and of the cloth she used on the lens. The letter lay face up on the third step from the bottom, squared to the edge of the tread as if someone had placed it with care. It was a plain cream envelope, a little furred at the corners with damp, and her name was written on it in ink. *Mrs M. Holt, The Light, Carrick Skerry.*

She didn't pick it up straight away. She stood with one hand on the cold iron rail and listened to the house. The wind had dropped in the night and the sea was doing its ordinary work on the rocks below, a slow breath in and out. She could hear the clock in the kitchen and the tick of the stove as it cooled, and nothing else. There had been nothing else for eleven years, apart from the boat and the men on it. Once, in the second winter, a gannet had come through the lantern glass in a storm and died on the floor among the shards.

She bent and took the letter. It weighed what a letter weighs.

In the kitchen she set it on the table beside the logbook and filled the kettle, because that was what came next. She wrote in the log: *0615. Light extinguished. Wind SW, light. Visibility good. Sea slight.* She looked at the word *slight* for a while. Then she sat down and turned the envelope over.

There was no return address. The flap was properly sealed, not tucked in. In the upper right corner of the front, where a stamp would go, there was no stamp. There was only a date, written in the same hand as her name: *13th November.*

Today was the twelfth. She knew it the way she knew the tides, without looking, but she looked anyway. The calendar from the chandler's in Ardmore hung by the door, with its picture of a trawler under an improbably blue sky. She had crossed off the eleventh last night before bed. The twelfth was clean.

The kettle began to tick and then to sing. She let it go a little longer than she needed to.

There were explanations. She arranged them as she made the tea, one beside another, the way she would lay out tools on a cloth. Someone might have misdated it. It might have come on last week's boat, tucked into a parcel, and shaken loose since. Callum, who skippered the boat and had a sense of humour she had never quite trusted, might have left it as a joke. Or she had put it there herself and forgotten. She liked that one least, so she held it longest, turning it over and testing its weight.

But there was the handwriting. She knew that too. She had known it on the stairs, and for the last quarter of an hour she had been carefully not knowing it. The long tail on the *H*. The *t* in *Light* crossed too high, as if the pen had lifted early, impatient for the next thing.

Upstairs she had a drawer of letters in that hand, tied with a bootlace because she had never owned ribbon. The last of them was dated nine years ago. She didn't need to fetch them to compare.

Outside, the light was coming up grey over the water, and the gulls had started on the eastern ledge. In an hour she would go out to check the rain gauge and the fog signal's air reservoir, then walk the circuit of the rock, as she did every morning. Every morning the rock was the same size.

She drank her tea and put the cup in the basin. She took the letter in both hands and held it the way you might hold something that could still be put back. Then she set it down again, unopened, against the sugar tin, with its date turned toward her.

It was addressed to tomorrow. She thought she could at least let it arrive.
