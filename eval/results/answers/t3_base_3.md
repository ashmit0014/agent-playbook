My recommendation is **no to a company-wide four-day week. Run a 3-month controlled pilot with the 15 developers only, and leave the support centre's schedule as it is for now.**

## Why

**1. The headline "40% productivity" figure doesn't hold up.** It comes from one company's Japan office, which closed on Fridays for a single month (August 2019). There was no comparison group, the numbers were self-reported in a press release, and the measure was sales per employee rather than output (source 03). The academic review says outright that this figure "should not be generalised" (05). The newsweekly, the blog and the HR magazine (01, 02, 06) all repeat the number without that context, so they can't support a decision. The blog is a promotional list and I gave it no weight.

**2. The better evidence is more modest.** The UK pilot covered 61 organisations and about 2,900 employees (04):
- 56 of the 61 kept the four-day week after the trial, but only 18 made it permanent.
- 71% of staff reported less burnout, and sick days went down.
- Revenue rose 1.4% on average, but only 23 firms supplied revenue data, and they did so voluntarily.
- Productivity was not measured directly, and the firms chose to take part.

Fairly read, this shows **wellbeing gains are reliable, and output roughly holds steady in the right settings.** It doesn't show a productivity boost.

**3. Most of our staff work in the setting where the evidence is weakest.** The review finds that in knowledge work, output per hour can rise enough to make up for fewer hours. In shift-based or customer-facing work, however, "total output or coverage fell unless staff were added" (05). The logistics firm in source 06 dropped its trial after six weeks because complaints rose.

**4. The support centre can't simply close one day a week.** 25 of our 40 staff must cover the phones 8am–6pm, Monday to Friday. Giving each of them a day off means that on any given day about a fifth of the team is out, roughly 5 people short. We would either lose coverage or need about 5–6 more hires to keep it, which works against the whole idea.

## What to propose to leadership

1. **Developer pilot, 3 months.** Developers work four days with no pay cut. Stagger the days off so someone is always available for incidents and for questions from support.
2. **Agree success measures before starting:**
   - delivery: features or story points shipped, cycle time and missed deadlines
   - quality: escaped bugs and incidents
   - staff: sick days, attrition and a short wellbeing survey
   
   Compare these against the previous 3 months.
3. **Support centre:** don't cut its days. Look separately at options that keep coverage, such as a compressed week (4 × 10 hours on rotating days, which fits the 8am–6pm window) or a nine-day fortnight on a rota. Model what each does to staffing before trialling anything.
4. **Plan for fairness.** If only developers get a four-day week, support staff may resent it. Say from the start that support is being looked at, and give a timeline.
5. **Decide at the end of the pilot.** Extend it, change it, or stop it based on the measures in step 2.

**One-line version for the meeting:** The evidence supports wellbeing benefits and says output can hold up in knowledge work, but not in phone-coverage roles. So pilot it with developers, measure it properly, and design something separate for support.
